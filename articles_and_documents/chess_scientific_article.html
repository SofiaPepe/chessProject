Methods

Sample

The study involved a total sample of 104 typically developing Italian children (56 boys, 48 girls), aged between 5 and 7 years (mean age = 6.07 years, SD = 0.62). Participants were recruited from the third year of two preschools (“D. Ricci” in Acquasparta and “G.S. Bernardo” in Narni) and from the first and second years of four primary schools (“A.B. Sabin” in Acquasparta, Montecchio Primary School, “Narni S. Lucia”, and “Narni Centro”). Children were randomly assigned to two experimental conditions: 57 (29 boys, 28 girls) joined the Experimental Chess Protocol Group (GSPS), while 47 (27 boys, 20 girls) were placed in the Control Group (CG). To ensure equivalence between groups, each experimental class was matched with a control class of the same school level, resulting in balanced age and group size distribution. After testing, children in the control group also participated in chess activities. Inclusion criteria were: native Italian speakers, no primary sensory deficits (visual or auditory), no neurological conditions, and no significant psychiatric or developmental disorders. We excluded To control for general cognitive functioning, all participants completed the Raven’s Coloured Progressive Matrices, a non-verbal measure of intellectual ability (@raven1995). Only children scoring within the normative range for their age group (percentile ≤ 5) were included. After this control, we have excluded 3 partecipants. None had prior structured chess experience. The study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee. Written informed consent was obtained from parents, and assent from each child.

Testing at t0 and t1

Children were individually tested in a quiet, empty room at their school. The GSPS group was assessed immediately before (T0) and after (T1) participating in the Giant Chess Game (GCG) training (Bocchi et al., 2022). The control group, which continued regular classroom activities without chess practice, was also tested at T0 and T1. The order of tests was counterbalanced for each participant.

Corsi Block-Tapping Test

The Corsi Block-Tapping Test (CBT; @monaco2013span; @corsi1972) is used to assess visuospatial working memory and short-term memory. The test uses a board (30 × 25 cm) with nine wooden blocks (3 × 3 cm) arranged in a scattered pattern. During the test, the examiner taps a sequence of blocks at a rate of one block every two seconds. The child is then required to reproduce the same sequence by tapping the blocks in the same order. The test begins with sequences of two blocks, and the length of the sequences increases progressively. For each sequence length (ranging from 2 to 9), five trials are administered. If the child correctly reproduces at least three out of five sequences for a given length, the next longer sequence is presented. If the child fails to reproduce at least three out of five sequences, the test is stopped. The span score corresponds to the number of blocks in the longest three sequences correctly repeated (maximum span = 9). Two parallel versions of the test were used, one for the pre-training (T0) and one for the post-training (T1) assessment.

Tower of London

The Tower of London (TOL; Shallice, 1982), included in the BVN 5-11 battery (Bisiacchi et al., 2005), assesses planning, problem-solving, and the ability to anticipate consequences. The task uses three pegs of different heights and colored balls. Children must reproduce a target configuration from a standard starting position, following rules: only one ball can be moved at a time, peg capacity cannot be exceeded, and the solution must use the fewest moves possible.

Moles Task

The Moles Task is a computerized child-friendly task adapted with moles as obstacles. It measures spatial memory, navigation skills, and visuospatial planning strategies. Children interact with an 8 x 8 grid on a computer screen using a mouse. The task includes three conditions designed to isolate and combine cognitive components: working memory, planning, and their integration (working memory plus planning).

  • In the working memory task children view a number of “moles” for 10 seconds, followed by a 5-second retention interval. They must recall and click the exact positions of the moles. Difficulty increases from 2 to 8 items.
  • In the planning test, children must trace the shortest path from start to finish, avoiding visible obstacles (moles). Difficulty increases with more obstacles.
  • In the combined test, obstacles disappear after 10 seconds, requiring children to remember their locations and plan the shortest path accordingly.

Teacher Questionnaires

The ABAS-II (Adaptive Behavior Assessment System, Second Edition; @harrison2003; @ferri2014) is a comprehensive measure of adaptive functioning from early childhood to adulthood. The Italian version of the ABAS-II Parent Form (5–21 years; Ferri et al., 2014) consists of 232 items, each rated on a scale from 0 (not able) to 3 (always/almost always). The parental form includes 10 skill areas—Communication, Community Use, Functional Academics, Home Living, Health and Safety, Leisure, Self-Care, Self-Direction, Social, and Work (the latter only for individuals aged 17 or older). These areas are combined to form three composite indices: Conceptual (CON; Communication, Functional Academics, Self-Direction), Social (SOC; Leisure, Social), and Practical (PRAC; Self-Care, Home Living, Community Use, Health and Safety), as well as an overall General Adaptive Composite (GAC). The Teacher Form was used to obtain ecological evaluations of children’s adaptive skills in the school context, providing standardized scores for individual domains and composite indices, including the GAC. For details about the psychometric properties of the Italian version, see Ferri et al. (2014). The BRIEF-2 (Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function, Second Edition; @gioia2015) is a revised questionnaire for the assessment of executive functions in daily life, developed to address some limitations of the original BRIEF. The BRIEF-2 includes nine clinical scales (Inhibit, Self-Monitor, Shift, Emotional Control, Initiate, Working Memory, Plan/Organize, Task-Monitor, Organization of Materials) and three composite indexes: BRI (Inhibit, Self-Monitor), ERI (Emotional Control, Shift), and CRI (Initiate, Working Memory, Plan/Organize, Task-Monitor, Organization of Materials). Items are rated on a 3-point scale (never, sometimes, always). The Teacher Form was standardized on a large sample of students aged 5 to 18 years, and confirmatory factor analyses supported a three-index model with strong internal reliability (coefficients: BRI = .95, ERI = .94, CRI = .98). The BRIEF-2 is widely used in both clinical and research settings to provide a comprehensive profile of executive functioning in children and adolescents.

Giant Chess Game Training

Experimental Procedure

The Giant Chess Game (GCG) consisted of 8 weekly chess lessons, each lasting 1 hour and divided into three work units. Lessons were conducted by a chess master with 15 years of experience, affiliated with FIDE (Fédération Internationale Des Échecs), the international governing body for chess. The large chessboard (2.5 m x 2.5 m) was divided into 8 rows and 8 columns. Rows of squares of the same color that coincide with the corners are called “diagonals”.

Work Unit 1

The activity begins with a familiarization phase using the giant chessboard (Fig. 3.6). Children are encouraged to explore the board freely, moving across it to actively discover its spatial features. Simple and gradual instructions are given regarding movements (e.g., one step forward, two steps back, one step to the right), aiming to make fundamental concepts such as squares, rows, and steps concrete and understandable. The teacher then introduces the concept of diagonals through structured play activities. For example, a game uses auditory signals: at the first clap, children stop moving; at the second clap, they must change the color of the square they are standing on. Practically, if a child is moving along black squares diagonally, at the double signal they must switch to a white square, and vice versa.

Work Unit 2

The teacher introduces the King, explaining its movement rules: the King can move one square at a time in any direction (forward, backward, right, left, and diagonally). To help children understand the different roles on the chessboard, they are paired and participate in a game called “Catch the King.” Both move as Kings, one step at a time, aiming to “capture” their partner by reaching their square. This activity dynamically reinforces movement rules and stimulates planning, inhibitory control, and anticipation of the other’s moves. A similar approach is used to introduce the Queen, who can move any number of squares in all directions (horizontal, vertical, diagonal). Learning occurs through problem situations requiring children to reason about the “control space” of the piece. During rule acquisition, “domain problems” are proposed, such as the classic challenge of placing eight Queens on the board so that none can “attack” another. While the full problem is complex, the version for children is mainly exploratory and educational: one child enters the board as the Queen and chooses a square; the next must place themselves avoiding all squares under the first Queen’s control, and so on.

Work Unit 3

The next phase focuses on the Knight, emphasizing its unique “L-shaped” movement (two squares in one direction and one perpendicular). To facilitate learning, a game called “Knight Race” is proposed, structured as a friendly competition between two children, each moving only as Knights, aiming to reach the opposite side of the board first. A key element emerges: the Knight cannot reach the opposite side in a straight line or with a rigid sequence of four moves. To make the final move without leaving the board, children are guided to change their body orientation, “turning” and changing direction. Through this experience, participants progressively understand that the Knight’s movement is not fixed in a single configuration but can be rotated and applied in various directions in space.

Results

Statistical analyses were performed using a reproducible Python pipeline. Preliminary analyses used independent-samples Welch t-tests to screen PRE scores for differences by sex and age. Training effects were then evaluated with age-adjusted ANCOVA models, with POST scores modeled as a function of the corresponding PRE score, group, and age. Robust HC3 standard errors were used for the ANCOVA models, and multiple comparisons were controlled with the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) procedure.

Time-based outcomes were logarithmically transformed before analysis to improve their suitability for linear models.

Training’s Effect on Cognitive Abilities

Before testing the effect of training, PRE scores were screened for possible demographic differences using Welch independent-samples t-tests. Sex differences were tested by comparing boys and girls on each PRE outcome. Age differences were tested with pairwise comparisons among the three age groups (5 vs. 6, 5 vs. 7, and 6 vs. 7 years). No sex-related difference survived FDR correction. Age-related differences survived FDR correction for seven PRE outcomes: log-transformed Planning execution time, log-transformed Planning total time, Raven accuracy, Tower of London rule violations, Moles Task working-memory accuracy, Moles Task working-memory span, and Moles Task working-memory trials. Because PRE performance differed by age, the pre-post training comparison was adjusted for age only. For each outcome, the ANCOVA model was POST ~ PRE + group + age; the group coefficient tested whether the experimental group improved more than the control group from T0 to T1 after accounting for baseline performance and age. Across the 45 PRE-POST outcomes tested, no group effect survived FDR correction in the age-adjusted models. The smallest adjusted q value was .260; this corresponded to Moles Task working-memory accuracy, which was nominally significant before correction (group beta = 0.85, 95% CI [0.25, 1.45], p = .006) but not significant after FDR correction. Thus, the analysis did not provide FDR-corrected evidence that the experimental group improved more than the control group between T0 and T1.

Relationship between autonomy and executive functions

To examine whether adaptive functioning and everyday executive functioning were associated with cognitive change, we tested whether ABAS-II and BRIEF-2 scores predicted individual treatment effectiveness across the cognitive outcomes. Predictor models were adjusted for age, and p values were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure. One ABAS-II scale survived FDR correction: higher supplemental Health and Safety scores predicted greater Tower of London accuracy effectiveness.

Overall, baseline Moles Task scores showed the expected developmental pattern: older children tended to achieve higher working-memory scores and faster planning performance. The strongest convergent associations linked Moles Task working-memory outcomes with Raven accuracy and Corsi backward span, and linked planning speed with Raven accuracy and adaptive communication/functional-academic ratings. Higher teacher-rated everyday executive difficulties on the BRIEF-2 were associated with lower planning accuracy and more non-optimal planning trials. These results support the interpretation that Moles Task performance reflects a combination of visuospatial working memory, non-verbal reasoning, everyday executive functioning, and age-related maturation.
